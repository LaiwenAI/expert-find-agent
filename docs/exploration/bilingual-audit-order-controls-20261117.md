# Bilingual audit order controls

The model-based audit of generated bilingual requests is not reliable enough to serve as a gold-label admission rule. A controlled experiment used 30 real construction cases across intent types and overlap strata, with both choice order and record order varied and two repeats per condition. It also used 12 synthetic controls with known faithful or deliberately altered constraints, evaluated over six choice permutations and two record orders. All 384 requests returned valid responses.

For the source-request grounding question, changing choice order changed 71 of 120 paired real-case answers; changing record order changed 59 of 120. Repeated calls with the same ordering differed in 16 of 120 comparisons. On the synthetic controls, grounding matched the designated answer in 73 of 144 requests. The equivalence question matched 138 of 144 synthetic controls. These tests expose sensitivity of this particular prompt and model interface, not the true error rate of all generated requests.

The grounding instruction should explicitly distinguish whether a derived request is supported by the original request from whether the original text proves a solution already exists. A revised instruction must be evaluated on held-out control templates and ordering permutations before it is used to select data. Independent human review remains necessary for expert-fit judgments and for measuring semantic equivalence in realistic cases.

The synthetic controls also vary the English view while leaving the Chinese view identical to the original request. Thus, their designated grounding answer implicitly requires the auditor to judge both views jointly. A future grounding test should specify which view is being judged and include separate changes to each view, as well as unsupported additions shared by both views. This avoids interpreting an ambiguity in the test target as a model error. The previous numerical results remain valid as measurements of the original joint-question protocol, not as estimates of isolated source-to-Chinese grounding accuracy.

An initial single-view English control uses six new templates with faithful and altered variants, each evaluated under all six choice orders. All 72 requests were valid and matched the designated label: 36 supported and 36 changed. Expected labels are excluded from the request payload. This is an easy synthetic control with repeated variants, so its 72 responses are not 72 independent examples and it cannot establish real-data accuracy. The earlier joint and new single-view experiments differ in cases and instructions; the difference cannot be attributed to one factor. Results are local in `artifacts/grounding-single-view-v1/`.

The single-view instruction was also applied from the Chinese source to the English derived request using the six earlier synthetic templates. All 72 requests across six permutations were valid and matched their designated labels (36 supported and 36 changed). These templates were already used for the previous audit and are not a held-out test; they do not establish performance on real requests, Chinese-to-Chinese grounding, or unsupported additions shared by both language views. Results are local in `artifacts/grounding-cross-language-v1/`.

Chinese-to-Chinese single-view controls were subsequently tested using six templates corresponding to the English single-view controls. All 72 requests across six permutations were valid and matched the designated labels. These are simple parallel examples, not independently sampled enterprise needs. Each of the three single-view cohorts contains only six underlying templates, with repeated permutations; their perfect observed results should not be generalized to the synthetic dataset. Results are local in `artifacts/grounding-chinese-v1/`.

For 30 selected real cases, the same single-view instruction was applied independently to each language, under two choice orders. All 120 requests were valid; three of 60 language-view pairs disagreed (all three English), and 117 of 120 answers were supported. Each order was called once, so call variation is confounded with order. These cases have no independent labels. Unlike the previous joint audit, this request supplies neither intent type nor cited spans and does not explicitly define allowed partial-intent omission. The two experiments therefore differ in input, target, prompt, and execution order; their disagreement rates cannot establish which change caused the difference. The strong support rate is not a quality estimate. Results are local in `artifacts/grounding-real-v1/`.

The three disagreements involve two evidence-lookup requests and one conditional-transfer request. In each, the original order produced supported and the reversed order produced changed or unsupported. Their source texts contain 298--526 characters and English derived requests 151--214 characters. This small selected subset motivates inspection of evidence scope and transfer conditions; it does not establish a category-wide or order effect.

An additional sequential repeat used the same frozen single-view protocol and produced 120 valid responses. Across rounds, 5 of 120 same-order paired answers changed. Within each round, 3 of 60 and 4 of 60 views respectively disagreed between the two orders. The runs were sequential rather than randomized contemporaneously, so provider or time variation remains confounded with repeat; no causal order effect follows. The two rounds contain no human ground truth. Paired results are in `artifacts/grounding-real-repeat-v1/paired-analysis.json`.

The local artifact `artifacts/bilingual-order-controls-v1/` preserves the protocol, cases, predictions and summary. Reproduction code is in `experiments/bilingual/audit_order_controls.py` and `experiments/bilingual/analyze_order_controls.py`.
